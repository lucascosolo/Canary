# D-02: Mass-atrocity and political-instability risk literature

- Date: 2026-10-20
- Agent: researcher (Sonnet, low effort), 13 tool uses

## Answer (verbatim; read 2026-10-20)

Evidence is partial. The publisher pages for Harff (Cambridge) and Goldstone (Wiley) returned 404 and 403, the EWP accuracy and methodology pages failed with "socket hang up", and I found no primary text for items 3, 5 and 8. What follows rests on search-snippet abstracts and secondary pages.

1. Harff 2003, APSR 97(1). The abstract says six factors jointly distinguish "with 74% accuracy" between internal wars and regime collapses that do and do not lead to genocide/politicide. The sample is 126 State Failure episodes from 1955 to 1997, of which 35 became geno/politicides. The factors, from a secondary news source, are prior genocides, upheaval since 1988, a minority elite, exclusionary ideology, regime type and trade openness. The regime factor is "type of regime", not "autocracy". I did not verify the factor list against the article itself. The 74% is classification of cases already in state failure, not a forecast of who enters it.
2. Goldstone et al. 2010, AJPS 54(1):190-208. The abstract reports over 80% accuracy two years ahead on 1955-2003 data, with regime type (a five-category Polity-based measure) as the strongest predictor. It reports institutions matter more than economics, demography or geography. I could not confirm infant mortality, neighbouring conflict, state-led discrimination or the "partial democracy with factionalism" category from the sources I could open. I found no critique, including Ulfelder's own caveats: not found.
3. Ulfelder's ensemble paper: not found. EWP's 2014 statistical-risk page says mass-killing onsets are rare, averaging fewer than two worldwide per year since WWII. It describes a three-model ensemble plus a crowd-sourced expert pool. The EWP accuracy page snippet says mean risk was about 8% in years with onsets and 3% in years without.
4. Straus 2015, Cornell UP. A review by Simon Turner says the negative cases are the main methodological strength. Its conclusion is that ideologies, leaders' agency and national "founding narratives" largely determine whether states answer rebellion with genocide. The book's chapters on Côte d'Ivoire ("Retreating from the brink") and Senegal ("Pluralism and accommodation") fit that reading. One reviewer criticises reliance on elite interviews taken at face value.
5. Verdeja 2016: not found, so the taxonomy and the critique of Stanton are unverified. The search returned only his 2019 paper, "Critical Genocide Studies and Mass Atrocity Prevention".
6. A JPR 2013 paper (50(4):437-452) describes an unconditional two-stage model of instability and genocide. It reports out-of-sample forecasts for 1988-2003 that caught 90.9% of genocide onsets and 79.2% of non-onset years. The snippet did not show authors, so I cannot confirm it is the Atrocity Forecasting Project paper. The Butcher et al. Targeted Mass Killing dataset (201 episodes, 1946-2017) is in J. Conflict Resolution 2020, not JPR. A 79% non-onset hit rate at a base rate under 2 per year implies many false positives (my inference, not computed from the paper).
7. Meyer, De Franco and Otto, Warning about War (CUP, 2019 online; some catalogues say 2020). They reframe the gap as a persuasion problem, not just an unwilling audience. The cases are Rwanda, Darfur, Georgia and Ukraine. This is consistent with response being the bottleneck, but I read no explicit "response not detection" sentence.
8. Sudan 2023 and Myanmar 2021 retrospectives: not found. EWP's 2016 assessment ranked Sudan and Myanmar in its top three. Its 2024-25 list had Chad first, Sudan second and Burma third. That is retrospective placement, not an assessment. The EWP guide says a coup attempt or civil-war onset can move a country sharply up the ranking.

## Caveats (verbatim)

None of the sources is a full paper text, and none of the DOIs was confirmed. Items 3, 5 and 8 were not established. The Harff factor list is secondary.

## Orchestrator note

The structural models (Harff, Goldstone, EWP) achieve high headline accuracy only because they rank countries by slow-moving structural risk, two years ahead, against a base rate of one or two onsets a year; their output is "which countries to watch", not "when". That is exactly the division of labour Canary should adopt: structural risk from published models (EWP's CSV is downloadable), near-term change from events and indicators, and the two shown side by side without being summed.
